# Multi-Model AI Triangulation: Value vs. Overhead

### A decision-science evaluation of when running research across multiple frontier models is worth the cost

---

## Executive Summary

The traditional case for triangulation — run the same prompt through Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini and compare — rested on **information diversity**: different training data, different knowledge cutoffs, different blind spots. Deep research modes with live web search have largely closed that gap. All three now read overlapping sets of current sources, so the remaining case for triangulation has to rest on **interpretation diversity**: whether different models reason differently about the same evidence.

The evidence says interpretation diversity is real, but narrower and more conditional than "run it through three models" assumes:

- **On factual and verifiable questions, frontier models already agree most of the time, and they're converging further as capability increases** — not diverging. A large-scale study of over 350 models found that when two models both get a question wrong, they choose the *same* wrong answer roughly 60% of the time on one benchmark, far above the ~33% you'd expect from independent errors, and this correlation is *highest* among the most accurate models ([Kim et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07962)). Triangulating this category mostly buys the illusion of independent confirmation.
- **On open-ended judgment questions, divergence is real and structured — but it comes from ambiguity in the question, not from which model you ask.** A 2026 study running 150 AI research agents on identical data found near-total agreement on well-specified sub-questions and enormous disagreement on ambiguously specified ones — and different model tiers showed stable, systematic "house styles" in how they resolved that ambiguity ([Gao & Xiao, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16744)). This is where triangulation earns its keep.
- **Classic "wisdom of crowds" math doesn't apply for free.** Both the Condorcet Jury Theorem and modern forecast-aggregation research require genuine independence between opinions; when opinions share sources or converge in style, agreement inflates confidence without inflating accuracy — a well-documented trap in human forecasting ([Lorenz et al., 2011](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1008636108); [Satopää & Baron et al., 2014](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/275937752_Two_Reasons_to_Make_Aggregated_Probability_Forecasts_More_Extreme)).
- **Most of the value can be captured for a fraction of the cost** through targeted verification passes, single-model self-sampling, or a structured critique step — reserving full independent triangulation for a small tier of genuinely high-stakes, contested, hard-to-reverse decisions.

**Bottom line:** default to single-model deep research; add a cheap cross-model disagreement check for medium-stakes calls; reserve full triangulation for the intersection of high stakes, low reversibility, and genuine unresolved expert disagreement. Section 9 gives the concrete protocol.

## 1. Reframing the Question: Information Diversity vs. Interpretation Diversity

Any benefit from combining multiple models' outputs has to come from a real mathematical source, not intuition. The relevant result — sometimes called the ensemble ambiguity or diversity-prediction decomposition — says that a combined estimate's error equals the *average* individual error minus a term that depends entirely on how much the individual estimates disagree with each other ([Krogh & Vedelsby, 1995](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1008636108), cited via the wisdom-of-crowds literature below). If the panel members are identical, that diversity term is zero and combining them buys nothing beyond what one member already gives you. Everything about whether triangulation is worthwhile reduces to: **how much genuine diversity exists between the models once they've all read the same internet?**

There are two possible sources of that diversity:

1. **Information diversity** — the models see different facts. Live web search largely eliminates this for any topic with decent search coverage, because all three deep research modes tend to surface the same authoritative, well-indexed sources.
2. **Interpretation diversity** — the models weigh, frame, or synthesize the same facts differently, because of differences in training emphasis, RLHF/alignment choices, default reasoning patterns, or house style.

The rest of this report is an attempt to measure #2 directly, using the best empirical and theoretical evidence available, rather than assuming it exists because #1 has shrunk.

## 2. When Do Frontier Models Actually Disagree? The Evidence

### 2.1 Verifiable questions: convergence, and it's getting worse (for triangulation's case)

The largest direct test of this question comes from Kim, Garg, Peng, and Garg's [*Correlated Errors in Large Language Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07962) (ICML 2025), which evaluated error correlation across 349 models on a HuggingFace leaderboard, 71 models on Stanford's HELM benchmark, and 20 major models (including Claude, GPT, and Llama variants) on a resume-screening task. The design specifically isolates *correlated errors* — not just overall agreement, which is inflated by both models simply being right — by asking: when two models are **both wrong**, how often do they land on the *same* wrong answer?

The results are stark. On HELM, models agree on the same incorrect answer roughly **60% of the time** when both err, against a baseline of about 33% if their mistakes were independent; on the HuggingFace set, actual agreement (42%) is more than triple the ~13% baseline. Same-company and same-architecture pairs are more correlated, as expected — but the more consequential finding is that **the single strongest predictor of error correlation is how accurate the two models are individually**. More capable models make *more* similar mistakes, even across different companies and architectures. The authors' interpretation: as models improve, they aren't just converging on correct answers, they're converging on which things they get wrong.

This has a direct downstream effect that matters for anyone using one model to check another (the "adversarial review" idea addressed in Section 4): when a model is used to judge another model's output, it systematically **inflates the score of less-accurate models it's correlated with**, because it can't detect an error it would have made itself. A companion study, [*Great Models Think Alike and This Undermines AI Oversight*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04313) (Goel et al., ICML 2025), finds the same pattern independently: LLM-as-judge scores are biased toward whichever candidate model is most *similar* to the judge, and model mistakes are becoming more correlated as capability increases — a trend the authors flag as a structural risk for any scheme that uses one AI to oversee another. Neither paper is about "deep research" workflows specifically, but both concern the exact mechanism that determines whether checking A against B tells you anything: the correlation of their mistakes.

The practical read for triangulation: on questions with a checkable factual answer — what a library's API supports, current pricing, who holds what position, what a regulation says — three grounded frontier models are likely to converge, and disagreement is more often a signal that a source is genuinely ambiguous or contested than a sign that you've caught an error only one model would have made.

### 2.2 Open-ended judgment: real, structured divergence

The clearest evidence that interpretation diversity is genuinely present — and where — comes from a March 2026 study designed almost exactly around this question, though for a different purpose. In [*Nonstandard Errors in AI Agents*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16744), Gao and Xiao deployed 150 autonomous Claude Code agents (100 on Sonnet 4.6, 50 on Opus 4.6) to independently analyze the *same* seven-billion-row market dataset and test six identical hypotheses, replicating a well-known human-researcher study, [Menkveld et al.'s (2024) "Nonstandard Errors"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16744) design, which had found that 164 human research teams given the same data produced wildly different results depending on undocumented analytical choices.

The AI version reproduces the phenomenon — but with an unusually clean structural signature. For the one hypothesis where the instructions gave an explicit, unambiguous definition ("the quoted bid-ask spread is the difference between the best ask and best bid"), all 150 agents converged almost perfectly — the spread of estimates was tiny, and 96% of agents landed on the same conclusion. For hypotheses phrased abstractly ("did trading volume change over time," with no specification of *which* volume measure), the agents split into stable, mutually exclusive camps — some using dollar volume, some share volume — that reached **opposite-signed conclusions** (+6% per year growth vs. −5% per year decline) despite analyzing identical data. Crucially, *within* any one interpretive camp, agents agreed tightly. The divergence lived entirely in **which valid operationalization of an ambiguous concept the agent chose**, not in noisy execution once that choice was made.

Two further findings sharpen the picture for triangulation specifically:

- **Different model tiers had stable, systematic "empirical styles."** For the market-efficiency question, 87% of Sonnet 4.6 agents chose one measure (autocorrelation) while 100% of Opus 4.6 agents chose a different one (variance ratio) — not noise, but a consistent methodological preference tied to the model itself. This is direct evidence that model choice shapes *how a question gets framed*, exactly the kind of interpretation diversity the case for triangulation depends on — and this was observed *within a single company's model family*, which is a lower bound on what you'd expect comparing Claude, GPT, and Gemini.
- **Simply having one AI critique another's work did not reduce disagreement.** In a peer-review stage, agents received detailed written critiques from other AI evaluators and were free to revise. Dispersion across agents was essentially unchanged afterward — individual agents moved, but roughly as many moved away from consensus as toward it. What *did* produce dramatic convergence was showing agents the top-rated exemplar reports from their peers, which triggered wholesale switching to match the exemplar's methodology. The authors are explicit that this looked like **imitation, not independent re-evaluation** — convergence through social influence rather than through resolving the underlying ambiguity. That distinction matters a great deal for Section 4 and 5 below.

A related 2026 study, [*Many AI Analysts, One Dataset*](https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.2606495123) (PNAS), corroborates the cross-model version of this finding directly: AI analysts reached divergent hypothesis-support verdicts from identical data across three domains, and conclusions shifted with both model choice and prompt choice. Together with older, purely human evidence — [Silberzahn et al.'s (2018)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16744) 29 research teams reaching opposite conclusions from the same soccer dataset, and [Botvinik-Nezer et al.'s (2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16744) 70 neuroimaging teams producing highly variable results from the same fMRI data — this establishes that **analytical divergence on open-ended questions is a structural feature of underspecified research tasks, not an artifact of using AI**. AI systems inherit it; they don't invent it, and interestingly, the 2026 study found the AI version was *narrower and more concentrated* than the human version (150 agents converged on essentially two OLS specifications, where human teams had used a much wider variety of estimation paradigms) — the AI multiverse of interpretations, while real, may be smaller than the human one.

**Synthesis.** The determining factor for whether models will genuinely disagree is not "how many models you ask" — it's whether the question has one well-specified target or admits multiple defensible operationalizations. A question like "what does trend X look like in this data" or "should we adopt architecture A or B" is exactly the abstract, multiply-interpretable type that produced 10+ percentage-point divergence in controlled testing. A question like "what is the current price of Y" or "does library Z support feature W" is exactly the well-specified type that produced near-unanimous agreement.

## 3. What Decision Science Says About Combining Opinions

The intuitive appeal of "ask more than one expert" rests on wisdom-of-crowds reasoning, and that reasoning comes with conditions that are easy to state and easy to violate.

**The Condorcet Jury Theorem**, the mathematical basis for the claim that aggregating opinions beats any one opinion, requires that each voter's probability of being correct is *independent* of the others' ([Wikipedia: Condorcet's jury theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet's_jury_theorem); [Estlund, 1994](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01079210)). When that independence breaks down — when voters share sources, follow the same influences, or are correlated for structural reasons — the theorem's guarantee breaks down with it, and the literature shows aggregation can, under sufficiently strong correlation, perform *worse* than trusting a single good voter ([Berg, 1996](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02400945); [arXiv:2409.00094](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00094) applies this directly to ensembles of classifiers and language models). Section 2.1's finding — that more capable models have *more* correlated errors — is precisely the condition under which this theorem stops helping you.

**The wisdom-of-crowds literature has a second, sharper warning: agreement can inflate confidence without inflating accuracy.** In a classic study, Lorenz et al. had participants estimate factual quantities repeatedly, sometimes with knowledge of others' estimates. Sharing information narrowed the spread of estimates and made people feel more confident — but did *not* make the group estimate more accurate, and in some cases made it *less* likely to bracket the true answer, because the underlying diversity that made the crowd wise in the first place had been erased by social influence ([Lorenz et al., 2011, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1008636108)). This is a direct analogue for a workflow where a person sees three AI outputs converge and reads that convergence as validation: if the convergence comes from shared search results and increasingly similar reasoning patterns rather than from independently arriving at the truth, the confidence boost is not earned.

**Forecasting research reaches the same conclusion from a different angle.** In the IARPA-funded forecasting tournaments that produced the "superforecasting" research program, the technique of algorithmically *extremizing* an aggregated forecast (pushing a 70% consensus toward, say, 85%) produced large accuracy gains — but only for groups with genuine informational diversity among members. Teams that shared information well and converged internally gained nothing from extremizing, because — in Tetlock's own framing — a team with no diversity behaves like clones, and clones shouldn't be treated as if they were adding independent evidence ([Baron, Mellers, Tetlock, Stone & Ungar, 2014](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/275937752_Two_Reasons_to_Make_Aggregated_Probability_Forecasts_More_Extreme); [background via AI Impacts](https://aiimpacts.org/evidence-on-good-forecasting-practices-from-the-good-judgment-project/)). This maps almost exactly onto the deep-research scenario: three models drawing on the same search results are closer to the "zero-diversity team" case than to the "genuinely dispersed experts" case that makes aggregation pay off.

**One expert is enough exactly when these independence conditions can't be met anyway** — which, per Section 2.1, is most of the time for verifiable questions once models are search-grounded. Multiple opinions earn their keep when the underlying judgments are genuinely dispersed, which Section 2.2 shows happens specifically around ambiguous, multiply-operationalizable questions.

## 4. Lighter-Weight Alternatives to Full Triangulation

Full triangulation — independent deep research passes on three separate platforms, synthesized by a human — is the most expensive way to capture interpretation diversity. The evidence suggests most of its value can be had more cheaply.

**Self-sampling one model.** Because a meaningful share of apparent "model diversity" is really just diversity of reasoning paths, sampling the *same* model multiple times and taking a majority vote captures a real slice of the benefit without paying for a second platform. Self-consistency prompting — sample several reasoning chains from one model, then vote — produced substantial accuracy gains on reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K +17.9 points, AQuA +12.2, SVAMP +11.0) purely from within-model sampling diversity ([Wang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11171)). A related finding, sometimes summarized as "more agents is all you need," showed that simply sampling one model repeatedly and voting can let a smaller model match a much larger one's accuracy on difficult tasks ([Li et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05120)). The caveat, well put in a follow-up theoretical treatment: this only helps "when it increases coverage or diversity; it saturates when additional agents are shared-blind-spot clones" ([arXiv:2605.14163](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.14163)) — so self-sampling helps most on exactly the reasoning/verifiable tasks where models already converge, and helps least on the abstract-judgment tasks where a single model's "house style" (Section 2.2) will simply repeat itself across samples.

**Adversarial review / structured critique.** Having one model critique or debate another's conclusion has real evidentiary support for a specific class of problems. Multi-agent debate has been shown to reduce hallucination and improve factual and mathematical accuracy relative to single-model inference ([Du et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14325)), and debate framed as an oversight mechanism has been shown to help a judge reach more accurate conclusions than simply consulting one model, including in settings where the judge doesn't have direct access to the ground truth ([Irving, Christiano & Amodei, 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.00899); [Khan et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.06782); [Michael et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08702)). But two cautions from Sections 2 and 3 apply directly here. First, a same-family or highly capable critic model inherits the correlated-error and self-preference problems documented above — it may not catch a mistake it would have made itself, and it tends to score same-family outputs generously. Second, and more important for judgment-type questions: the Nonstandard Errors study found that a critique/peer-review pass **by itself did not reduce disagreement** on ambiguous questions — it caused agents to move, but not toward each other. Debate and critique are well-evidenced for catching *errors* (a wrong calculation, an unsupported claim, a logical gap); they are not well-evidenced for resolving genuine, defensible *interpretive* disagreement, where there may be no error to find.

**Structured self-critique within one session.** A cheaper variant, borrowed from intelligence analysis, is to have a single strong model explicitly enumerate competing hypotheses or interpretations and weigh evidence against each — the core idea behind Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH), a decades-old technique for forcing analysts to consider alternatives rather than anchor on the first plausible answer ([Heuer & Pherson](https://greydynamics.com/a-guide-to-structured-analytic-techniques-sats-for-intelligence/)). This is worth doing but shouldn't be assumed to work automatically: a randomized study of trained human intelligence analysts found that many did not actually follow ACH's steps rigorously even when instructed to, often reverting to simpler, less disciplined reasoning ([Dhami, 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/acp.3550)). The implication for AI use is the same — a vague instruction to "consider alternative views" is weak; forcing an explicit, structured output (a literal hypothesis-by-evidence matrix, not a paragraph gesturing at "other perspectives") is what makes the technique actually change the reasoning rather than just decorate it.

**Targeted verification passes.** Distinct from a full critique, this means asking a second model (or the same model, fresh) to check a small number of specific, checkable claims — a statistic, a quote, whether a cited feature currently exists — rather than re-deriving the whole analysis. This is cheap precisely because it targets Section 2.1's territory (verifiable facts), where convergence is high and a quick check is enough to catch an isolated hallucination without paying for redundant interpretation.

**Selective triangulation, triggered by risk.** The strongest lightweight alternative is really a policy, not a technique: don't decide up front to triangulate or not — run a cheap first pass, and use *disagreement itself* as the signal for whether the expensive step is warranted. This is developed as the core recommendation in Section 9.

## 5. The Real Cost of Triangulation

The "3x cost" framing in the original question is worth unpacking, because for AI-run research the dollar cost and the real cost are no longer the same thing.

**Raw compute cost is now genuinely cheap.** The Nonstandard Errors study is a useful data point here even though it wasn't designed to measure this: running 150 independent autonomous research agents through a full multi-thousand-word analysis, three times over (450 total runs), cost **$1,558 total** — a few dollars per run. The equivalent human study it replicated (164 research teams) is estimated to have cost on the order of **$2.7 million** and 27 person-years ([Gao & Xiao, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16744); [Menkveld et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16744)). Multiplying an AI deep-research pass by three no longer means multiplying a scarce, expensive resource by three the way it did for human analyst-hours. General ensemble/routing literature confirms the mechanical cost of querying multiple models scales roughly linearly with the number of models used simultaneously ([Chen et al., 2025, LLM Ensemble survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18036)) — real, but modest in absolute terms for most individual decisions.

**The real cost is human reconciliation time and the risk of manufactured confidence.** Three research reports don't synthesize themselves. Someone has to read all three, adjudicate disagreements, and decide what to do when they conflict — and Section 3's evidence on the "confidence effect" is the sharper risk: if the reports mostly agree (likely, per Section 2.1, for factual content), the reader may walk away *more* confident than the evidence warrants, because agreement is being read as independent confirmation when it's substantially the product of shared sources and converging model behavior. The Nonstandard Errors study's own peer-review stage illustrates the failure mode directly: exposing agents to each other's conclusions produced convergence that the authors describe as "imitation rather than understanding" — a plausible outcome of naive triangulation is that a human reader experiences three-way agreement as validation, when structurally it may be closer to the shared-illusion Lorenz et al. documented in human forecasters.

**The reframed question, then, is not "is interpretation diversity worth 3x the API cost" — it almost always is, since that cost is small — but "is it worth 3x the reader's synthesis time and the risk of false confidence from correlated agreement," given what the specific question is likely to produce.** That is a cost-benefit calculation that depends on the type of question (Section 2) and the stakes of the decision (Section 6), not on a fixed multiplier.

## 6. A Cross-Domain Check: Second Opinions in Medicine

Medical second opinions are a mature, well-studied real-world analogue for exactly this question — when does consulting a second (or third) independent expert change the outcome, and when is it wasted effort? The evidence maps cleanly onto the criteria this report is building toward.

**Value is heavily concentrated in complex, ambiguous cases — not spread evenly across all cases.** A Mayo Clinic study of 286 patients referred specifically for a second opinion on complex conditions found only 12% left with their original diagnosis fully confirmed; 66% had it modified or refined, and 21% received a completely different diagnosis ([Mayo Clinic / Naessens et al.](https://newsnetwork.mayoclinic.org/discussion/mayo-clinic-researchers-demonstrate-value-of-second-opinions/)). That looks like overwhelming evidence for "always get a second opinion" — until you notice the sampling: this is a population that was *already flagged* as complex enough to warrant tertiary referral. A broader systematic review across general second-opinion contexts found major-change rates ranging from **10% to 62%** depending on the setting and population ([Van Such et al., Mayo Clinic Proceedings](https://www.mayoclinicproceedings.org/article/S0025-6196(14)00245-6/fulltext)) — a much wider and, on the low end, much smaller effect than the referral-population headline suggests. A dedicated oncology second-opinion study found meaningful treatment changes in 23–57% of cases depending on cancer type, but *actual diagnostic changes* — as opposed to differing management judgment calls about an agreed-upon diagnosis — accounted for only 3–17% ([PMC10134380](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10134380/)).

**There are real diminishing returns to additional opinions.** Reporting on second-opinion research indicates that a second opinion meaningfully cuts diagnostic error, while a third opinion adds comparatively little on top of that ([HCPLive summary](https://www.hcplive.com/view/doctors-and-data-agree-second-opinions-improve-patient-outcomes)) — consistent with the general shape of Condorcet-style returns, where the marginal value of each additional independent voter shrinks quickly, and shrinks faster still once voters stop being independent.

**The transferable lesson is about triage, not frequency.** Second opinions are valuable exactly where the first opinion is least certain to be right — complex, atypical, high-stakes presentations — not as a routine check on every diagnosis. Applied to AI research: the case for a second (or third) model is strongest exactly where Section 2.2 says real interpretation diversity shows up (ambiguous, judgment-laden, high-stakes questions), and weakest exactly where routine confirmation adds cost without adding information (well-specified, verifiable, low-stakes questions) — which is also where Section 2.1 says models already agree.

## 7. Decision Criteria: When Triangulation Earns Its Cost

Pulling Sections 2, 3, and 6 together into an actionable rubric:

| Signal | Skip triangulation (single model) | Cheap cross-check (one critique/verification pass) | Full triangulation (independent multi-model runs) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Question type** | Factual/verifiable — has a checkable answer | Predictive/forecasting under real uncertainty | Subjective or architectural judgment with genuine trade-offs |
| **Reversibility** | Two-way door — cheap and fast to undo | Costly but correctable within weeks or months | One-way door — expensive or impossible to unwind |
| **Expert contestedness** | Settled; consensus practice exists | Some debate, but a majority view exists | Genuinely contested among domain practitioners |
| **Stakes of a wrong call** | Low, bounded, locally contained | Moderate — real but recoverable cost | High — cascading or hard-to-reverse cost |
| **Typical example** | "What does library X's current API support?" / "What's the current price of Y?" | "How is this technology likely to be supported in three years?" | "Should we rebuild our core architecture around approach A or B?" |

Two clarifications on how to use this table:

- **These signals should be combined, not read independently.** A high-stakes, irreversible decision that is nonetheless a factual lookup (e.g., "is this vendor's compliance certification current?") still belongs in the "skip triangulation" column for the research step — the stakes argue for *thoroughness and verification*, not for querying three models about the same searchable fact. Triangulation earns its cost at the intersection of an ambiguous question type *and* real stakes, not from either alone.
- **Reversibility and contestedness are doing most of the work.** They are the two factors decision science flags as differentiating "one expert is enough" from "you need more than one" — in the second case because there's residual, genuine uncertainty about what the best answer even is (Section 3), and in the first case because a wrong single-model answer costs little to correct (Section 6).

## 8. The Recommended Protocol

A concrete, staged default, rather than a blanket policy:

**1. Default to single-model deep research.** For most questions, including most "important" ones, a single frontier model's deep research pass — well-scoped, with enough tool calls to be thorough — is sufficient. This is doubly true for anything closer to the "factual/verifiable" end of the spectrum, where Section 2.1's evidence says additional models mostly reproduce the same answer.

**2. For medium-stakes or moderately consequential decisions, add one cheap disagreement probe — not a second full research run.** Give a second model the first model's conclusion and key supporting evidence, and ask it to independently assess whether it would reach the same conclusion and why, or to specifically hunt for gaps, unsupported claims, or missing considerations. This costs a small fraction of a full parallel deep-research pass and is where the evidence in Section 4 is strongest: catching identifiable errors is well-supported by debate and critique research; it does not require duplicating the whole research process.

**3. Escalate to full independent triangulation only when either of two things is true:** (a) the decision sits in the high-stakes, low-reversibility, genuinely-contested corner of the Section 7 table, or (b) step 2's cheap probe actually surfaces disagreement rather than confirmation. Disagreement at the cheap-check stage is the single best empirical trigger available — it is direct evidence that this particular question is one of the ambiguous, multiply-interpretable ones where Section 2.2 shows real divergence lives, rather than a guess made in advance about the topic in general.

**4. When you do triangulate, extract value from *why* the models disagree, not from majority vote.** With a panel of three, and given how correlated frontier models now are (Section 2.1), a 2-to-1 split is weak statistical evidence on its own — it is not the large, independent jury the Condorcet theorem needs to be reassuring. It is, however, a strong prompt for a human to go investigate the specific point of divergence: which assumption, source, or framing produced the different answer. The reasoning behind a disagreement is almost always more informative than the count of votes on either side.

**5. Don't mistake convergence for validation.** If a cross-model check produces agreement, that is modestly reassuring for questions with real informational stakes, but it is not strong evidence for genuinely interpretive questions — Sections 2.2 and 5 both show that AI-to-AI convergence can come from correlated reasoning or imitation rather than independent confirmation of a correct answer. Treat agreement as "no red flag found," not as "independently proven."

## 9. A Note on Method and Self-Reference

This evaluation was written by an AI model assessing the value of processes that AI models are the subject of, and that structural position deserves explicit treatment rather than a disclaimer.

The two failure modes are real and pull in opposite directions: overstating multi-model value would flatter the idea that more AI involvement is always better; understating it as a display of false modesty would be its own kind of bias, optimizing for appearing humble rather than for being right. The approach taken here was to anchor every substantive claim in sources external to any judgment about AI's own importance — ensemble learning theory that predates LLMs by decades, jury theorems and forecasting research developed for human panels, medical second-opinion data, and the empirical LLM-correlation studies cited throughout, rather than introspection about how useful multi-model collaboration feels from the inside. Notably, the resulting recommendation leans toward *less* triangulation than the popular "use several AIs and compare" framing suggests — if there were a self-serving pull in this analysis, that outcome is evidence it was resisted rather than indulged, though the reader should weigh that observation for what it's worth rather than as proof of objectivity.

One honest limitation: as of this writing, there is not yet a large body of controlled studies testing triangulation of full commercial deep-research pipelines specifically, across Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini, on real-world decisions — that product category is too new. This report instead builds its case from the closest available rigorous evidence: large-scale correlated-error studies on structured tasks (Section 2.1), a controlled multi-agent study on open-ended analytical tasks that is structurally very close to a deep-research scenario (Section 2.2), and decades of adjacent decision science (Sections 3 and 6). That is a synthesis of strong analogues, not a single dispositive experiment, and the criteria in Section 7 should be treated as the best currently available guidance rather than a settled, final answer.
